# Thinking Frameworks

## Critical Thinking

### Definition
Critical thinking involves evaluating reasoning against intellectual standards to eliminate bias and improve judgement (Paul and Elder, 2014). Rather than accepting information passively, it is a goal-directed, reflective process that combines analytical abilities with open minded mental dispositions to decide what to believe or do (Fisher, 2001; Hitchcock, 2022).

### How to Use It
1. **Break Down the Reasoning:** Identify the core elements of an issue like purpose, key question, evidence, assumptions, inferences and implications (Paul and Elder, 2014).
2. **Test the Evidence & Sources:** Separate main conclusions from supporting reasons, check source credibility and evaluate alternative explanations (Fisher, 2001).
3. **Apply Quality Standards:** Check your thinking for clarity, accuracy, relevance, logic, and fairness while staying willing to reconsider your view (Paul and Elder, 2014; Hitchcock, 2022).

### Real-Life Application
* **Evaluating Online News or Claims:** Instead of sharing a viral post immediately, check who published it, verify the underlying evidence, and look for hidden biases or logical flaws (Fisher, 2001; Paul and Elder, 2014).
* **Troubleshooting Everyday Problems:** When diagnosing an issue such as figuring out the cause of an unexpected symptom or a sudden delay,avoid jumping to the first obvious conclusion; instead, list possible causes and test each against observable facts (Hitchcock, 2022).
* **Making Major Decisions:** Before committing to a job offer or purchase, list your underlying assumptions, weigh the long-term implications, and deliberately consider opposing viewpoints (Paul and Elder, 2014).

## Creative Thinking

### Definition
Creative thinking requires generating ideas or products that are different from others and useful in real life (Runco and Jaeger, 2012). Under this standard two part definition, true creativity demands both **originality** (novelty or uniqueness) and **effectiveness** (practical utility, appropriateness, or value), because an unusual idea that serves no purpose is merely random rather than creative (Runco and Jaeger, 2012). Rather than being a rare talent, it is a deliberate mindset and set of repeatable techniques used to break past conventional habits and uncover fresh possibilities (Durmonski, 2022).

### How to Use It
1. **Frame Challenges as Questions:** Turn daily frustrations or goals into specific questions such as asking what is too complicated or what wears you out to focus your mind on meaningful problems worth solving (Durmonski, 2022).
2. **Reverse the Obvious:** List your core assumptions about a product, service, or routine and deliberately flip them (e.g., a restaurant with no menu or paying for time instead of food) to force unconventional solutions (Durmonski, 2022).
3. **Cross-Pollinate & Combine:** Connect unrelated fields through analogies or break a problem down into smaller parts to recombine existing ideas in fresh ways (Runco and Jaeger, 2012; Durmonski, 2022).
4. **Filter for Effectiveness:** After generating novel ideas without self-censorship, evaluate them against real-world constraints to ensure they are practical, appropriate and genuinely useful (Runco and Jaeger, 2012).

### Real-Life Application
* **Product & Service Innovation:** Reverse standard industry rules such as letting customers test-drive a car or build a website before paying to remove friction and stand out from competitors (Durmonski, 2022).
* **Everyday Problem-Solving:** When struggling to stick to a home workout routine, identify the exact barriers (lack of gear or time) and combine a pre-packed equipment bag with 10-minute routines to make starting effortless (Durmonski, 2022).
* **Content Creation & Design:** Approach a familiar topic from an unexpected angle to capture attention, while ensuring the final output remains accurate, relevant, and useful to your audience (Runco and Jaeger, 2012).

## Mathematical Thinking

### Definition
Rather than simply memorising standard procedures or executing routine calculations, mathematical thinking focuses on identifying and analysing patterns in numbers or behaviours to solve problems (Devlin, 2012). It is the ability to think and make judgments independently by determining which methods to apply, abstracting complex situations and using logical reasoning to tackle unfamiliar problems (Katagiri, 2004; Devlin, 2012).

### How to Use It
1. **Determine the Right Approach First:** Before jumping into calculations or tools, clarify the meaning of the problem and decide which operations or methods are actually needed (Katagiri, 2004).
2. **Look for Patterns & Abstract:** Strip away surface details to spot recurring structures, represent relationships clearly and simplify complex steps to economise thought and effort (Devlin, 2012; Katagiri, 2004).
3. **Test Conditions & Reason Logically:** Change variables or problem conditions (functional and analogical thinking) to see if your solution still holds and verify conclusions step by step from clear definitions (Katagiri, 2004; Devlin, 2012).

### Real-Life Application
* **Comparing Pricing & Group Deals:** Instead of recalculating every scenario from scratch, simplify the underlying ratio (e.g., comparing group discount rates against individual ticket prices) so you can instantly find the break even point for any group size (Katagiri, 2004).
* **Software Engineering & Algorithms:** Rather than writing repetitive, hardcoded rules, identify the underlying data pattern and design a general, scalable algorithm (Devlin, 2012).
* **Data Analysis & Forecasting:** Look past isolated daily numbers to spot structural trends, test what happens when key variables change and uncover causal relationships in user behaviour or business metrics (Devlin, 2012; Katagiri, 2004).

## Scientific Thinking

### Definition
Scientific thinking involves actively pursuing knowledge through inquiry, logical reasoning and evidence evaluation rather than depending only on formal lab experiments (Kuhn, 2011). It is a self-directed, self-corrective mode of thought in which you coordinate theory with evidence and apply rigorous intellectual standards such as clarity, accuracy, precision and objectivity to quantify, explain and predict how things work (Kuhn, 2011; Paul and Elder, 2015).

### How to Use It
1. **Define the Question & Hypothesis:** Formulate a clear, precise question about an observed phenomenon and treat every proposed explanation as a supposition until tested (Paul and Elder, 2015; Kuhn, 2011).
2. **Design Controls & Isolate Variables:** Control surrounding conditions and test one factor at a time so you can separate genuine cause and effect relationships from coincidence (Kuhn, 2011; Paul and Elder, 2015).
3. **Evaluate Data Objectively:** Gather accurate, relevant information, explain any data that contradicts your main conclusion and update your assumptions when the evidence demands it (Kuhn, 2011; Paul and Elder, 2015).

### Real-Life Application
* **Troubleshooting Tech or Habits:** When debugging slow code or identifying what triggers poor sleep, control your environment and change one variable at a time (e.g., disabling one plugin or cutting late caffeine) to isolate the true cause (Kuhn, 2011; Paul and Elder, 2015).
* **Evaluating Health, Nutrition, or Product Claims:** Look beyond anecdotal stories or pseudoscientific claims by checking whether repeatable, controlled evidence actually supports the conclusion before changing your routine (Kuhn, 2011; Paul and Elder, 2015).
* **Running Business or Personal Experiments:** Treat new study methods, workflows, or marketing ideas as testable hypotheses—measure clear before-and-after metrics and account for contrary results rather than relying on gut instinct (Kuhn, 2011; Paul and Elder, 2015).
